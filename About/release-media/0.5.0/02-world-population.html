A world of millions that costs almost nothing

01

Nobody is stored

The world keeps proportions, not people: a tile's make-up — xenotypes, ages, beliefs, wealth — is a formula of the world seed and the land, recomputed on demand. A city of six thousand costs exactly what a homestead of a hundred does: nothing.

02

People are made only when needed

The usual way round is a database with a row per person, aggregated upward. This is the inverse: an individual is drawn from the local mix only when a raid, a trader or a recruit actually needs a face — then forgotten. No census, ever.

03

The world remembers only what changed

This is what makes it cheap enough to live. Every place starts from its seed-derived baseline for free. Once a city grows or a region drains, it stores one number — how far it has moved — and a timestamp. Everywhere untouched keeps costing nothing.

Cities grow by pulling on the country

A settlement's target is the pull of the land around it — its attraction, read fresh whenever anyone looks. Its current size chases that target: fast when the gap is wide, easing as it closes. That is the whole growth curve, and it needs no day‑by‑day simulation — just one stored number caught up in closed form the moment a region is opened.

What a populated world unlocks · 0.5.0

A living population, not a backdrop

With people actually on the land, the world stops being scenery and starts behaving like a place with its own life — one you can read on the map and feel in who shows up at your gate.
